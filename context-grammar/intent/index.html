Definition

Intent names the human aim behind the moment.

Intent is Stage 1 of the Context Grammar Decision Pipeline: the structured aim a person is trying to fulfill in a specific situation.

It can be short, like "get water now." It can also be part of a longer arc, like "keep my family organized this week." What makes it Intent is not its length. It is that the system can read the aim in context and support it without reducing it to a raw command string.

Intent is not a persona or demographic segment. It changes with time, place, device, social setting, physical state, and permission. A person can have one durable goal and several different Intents inside the same day.

Context Grammar treats Intent as a structured runtime object. It may come from a direct command, a visible action, a repeated pattern, or an ambient state the environment should respect.

Intent v2: two orthogonal axes.

Detection Channel describes how the intent surfaces. Awareness Depth describes how conscious the intent is. The two axes are independent: an Ambient signal can carry a Stated, Inferred, or Latent intent.

Detection Channel (Axis 1)

  • Explicit — user says, taps, or types it
  • Active Implicit — current action reveals intent
  • Passive Implicit — pattern + moment reveals it
  • Ambient Implicit — continuous background state

Awareness Depth (Axis 2)

  • Stated — clearly known and expressed
  • Inferred — readable from context
  • Latent — emerging, not yet consciously formed

The interesting frontier: the Latent column

Most of the 4×3 matrix falls on the obvious diagonal. The Latent column is the design challenge: these are intents the user has not yet formed consciously. They require the Negotiation Layer — never direct action.

Stated Inferred Latent
Explicit "Set a 20-min timer" (majority) rare "I've been tired lately" → underlying lifestyle redesign need
Active Implicit rare Running gear on → going for a run Early-morning lights on → insomnia/anxiety signal
Passive Implicit — Friday 7pm pizza pattern Step count halved in 6 months → burnout precursor
Ambient Implicit — 11pm in bed reading → sleep mode Persistent family-dinner-time drift → relationship erosion

Hard rule: Latent Intent must always pass through the Negotiation Layer. It is never acted on directly, regardless of Gate confidence score.

Quick overview

Start with four useful distinctions.

01

Command is not enough

Many products wait for text or speech. Human service often understands the need before the request is spoken.

02

Intent has forms

Explicit, Active, Passive, and Ambient signals give implementation teams a sharper taxonomy than "user asked."

03

Inference needs memory

Tokens describe now. Brains supply history. Coordinator compares them and estimates confidence.

04

Confidence governs action

A high-confidence Intent still passes through Autonomy and Disclosure before the system acts.

Metaphor

Two restaurants. Two kinds of AI.

At an ordinary restaurant, the server brings water after you ask. That is Explicit Intent: a stated need followed by a response.

At a restaurant that knows you, the server sees the spicy dish arrive and quietly refills your glass. They read the situation against their memory of you. That is Implicit Intent: a need understood before it is spoken.

Most AI still behaves like the ordinary restaurant. Context Grammar describes how to design the second kind, while keeping permission and control visible.

Two forms

Explicit Intent is a command. Implicit Intent is a reading of the moment.

Explicit

Declared clearly

The user says, taps, or types what they want: "Set a timer," "Call Mom," "Buy Sota soccer shoes." The system can act with low inference because the request is explicit.

Implicit

Understood from context

Nothing is said. Tuesday morning, uniform by the door, tight shoes, and a match on Saturday together create a likely Intent. The system should express it as a proposition plus confidence.

Scenario

Tuesday morning: Sota's shoes are too tight.

"Mom, my soccer shoes are too tight!"

Tokens register the current situation

Sota has not said a word. The home reads foot discomfort, morning time pressure, family-only social exposure, and a visible refrigerator display that can carry a compact prompt.

Multiple Brains add memory

Person Brain knows Sota's age, sport, and recent growth. Household Brain remembers two recent shoe complaints and a previous late replacement. Calendar Brain knows practice is today and the match is Saturday.

Coordinator connects the evidence

Foot discomfort plus Tuesday soccer plus recent growth plus a match in five days becomes a proposition: "Sota likely needs new soccer shoes before Saturday." Confidence is high because independent Brains agree.

Explicit Intent confirms the inference

When Sota speaks, the system does not start from zero. His sentence confirms the reading and increases confidence, but the contextual work has already been done.

Mai gets a low-friction action

One line appears on her iPhone: "Sota's soccer shoes — outgrown. Match Saturday. Tap to auto-order ($48, EU 37)." She does not need to research sizes, check the calendar, or remember the last order.

Architecture

Intent needs memory, but not one giant memory.

Person Brain

One per family member. It separates Identity, Learning, and Now, and identifies who the Intent belongs to.

Household Brain

Shared history, purchase patterns, budget priorities, unresolved tasks, and family-level context that no single person owns alone.

Calendar Brain

Family calendar, school schedules, work travel, practice days, and match dates. It turns a possible need into an urgent one.

Why separate Brains matter

Separation gives Disclosure Dial real boundaries. Sota's personal data can stay in his Person Brain. Household finances can stay in the Household Brain. Calendar urgency can be consulted without exposing unrelated private details.

One merged memory makes those lines hard to enforce. Multiple Brains let Coordinator form Intent while still respecting ownership, permission, and domain boundaries.

Detection Channel

Detection Channel: four implementation-ready forms

Type 1

Explicit Intent

The user states what they want by voice, tap, or type. No inference is needed.

Type 2

Active Intent

The user says nothing, but their current action reveals what they want: running gear plus earphones plus phone pickup.

Type 3

Passive Intent

The moment matches a learned pattern: Friday 7pm, fridge empty, family usually orders pizza.

Type 4

Ambient Intent

A continuous background state the environment should respect: reading in bed at 11pm means calm, warmth, dim light, and no interruptions.

These four channels define Axis 1 (Detection). Axis 2 (Awareness Depth) is orthogonal: each channel can carry Stated, Inferred, or Latent content depending on what the signal reveals about the user's consciousness of their own goal.

Intent vs JTBD

JTBD explains a durable job. Intent names the aim inside a live situation.

Framework

Jobs-to-be-Done

A durable job the user hires a product to help with: stay organized as a parent, stay fit without scheduling workouts, or reduce household admin. It is useful for product strategy.

Runtime

Intent

The human aim as it appears in context. It may overlap with a JTBD, or it may be much smaller: "pizza, probably, ask me," or "running in five, do not interrupt." It drives runtime interpretation.

Formula

Implicit Intent in one line.

Implicit Intent (t) = Coordinator(Tokens(t), Person Brain(subject), Household Brain, Calendar Brain, Disclosure Dial) -> { proposition, confidence }

The output is not a bare claim. It is a proposition with confidence and evidence, and that confidence feeds Autonomy Dial stage selection.

At 92%, Sota's shoes still get a one-tap confirmation, not a silent purchase. At 50%, the system should avoid interrupting and may only write a weak pattern to memory. Low-confidence inference should stay low-cost.

How it connects

Intent is the want. Tokens shape delivery.

Layer Role Example
Intent What is wanted. New soccer shoes before Saturday.
Tokens What is true about the moment. Morning rush, high cognitive load, fridge display available, budget feasible, autonomy requires confirmation.

Common questions

Questions hiring readers usually ask.

Is Intent the same as a goal?

No. A goal or JTBD names a broader job: "lose weight this year." Intent names the aim as it appears in context: "I need a low-friction dinner tonight," or "do not interrupt this run." Goals shape strategy. Intent guides runtime interpretation.

Is Implicit Intent creepy?

It can be, unless permission and control are designed in. Disclosure Dial defines what the system may know. Autonomy Dial defines how far it may act. Inference should happen only where permission is granted.

Where is Intent stored?

Intent itself is not stored as a permanent profile. It is interpreted from the current situation. Patterns learned from Intent are stored in the right Brain's Learning Layer: shoe growth in Person Brain, family replacement timing in Household Brain, Tuesday morning load in Calendar Brain.

Why not merge all Brains into one?

Because merging collapses privacy and ownership boundaries. A child's health context, household finances, and calendar urgency need different sharing rules. Separate Brains let Disclosure Dial control which information moves where.
